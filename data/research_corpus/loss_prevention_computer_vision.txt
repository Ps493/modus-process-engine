Computer vision is becoming the primary technology upgrade path for retail
loss prevention, converting existing security camera infrastructure into
real-time monitoring platforms rather than after-the-fact review tools.
Industry analysis (BizTech Magazine, citing Forrester and IDC) reports
that IDC projects roughly half of large retailers will expand computer
vision for store monitoring by 2028, with associated shrinkage reductions
around 40%. The mechanism is consistent across vendors: cameras positioned
over self-checkout lanes cross-reference scanned items against items
physically placed in the bag, flagging mismatches (missed scans, barcode
swapping, concealment) in near real time — industry reporting cites
roughly a two-second alert latency — and routing the flagged event to a
human attendant for a polite, non-confrontational intervention rather than
having the system act autonomously. This human-in-the-loop pattern is
consistent across every vendor reviewed. Self-checkout is specifically
called out as the highest-shrink checkout format, reportedly losing
significantly more per dollar than staffed lanes, which is why it is the
first place retailers deploy this technology. Beyond fraud detection, the
same camera infrastructure is increasingly reused for adjacent operational
purposes — queue-length monitoring feeding into staffing/scheduling
systems, and automated incident-ticket creation with supporting video
clips that significantly reduces manual investigation time. Privacy design
is a recurring theme: several vendors explicitly avoid biometric/facial
recognition data, instead using anonymised behavioural and item-level
detection to reduce regulatory exposure (e.g., under biometric privacy
laws such as BIPA) while still achieving the fraud-detection outcome.
