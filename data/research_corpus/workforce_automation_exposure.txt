Research compiled from PwC's Global AI Jobs Barometer, McKinsey Global
Institute, and the World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report indicates
that automation exposure varies sharply by task structure rather than by
industry alone. Roles built on routine, predictable, codifiable tasks with
well-defined inputs and outputs — data entry, invoice/document processing,
basic customer service scripting, scheduling, and reconciliation — show
the highest displacement risk, while roles requiring judgement in
ambiguous situations, relationship management, creative synthesis, or
physical presence show materially lower risk. Notably, PwC's research
found that employment has continued growing even in highly AI-exposed
occupations (though at a slower rate than less-exposed occupations),
suggesting the near-term effect is more about task-level automation and
role redefinition than wholesale elimination of job categories. McKinsey
research cited in workforce studies estimates that organisations adopting
AI at scale expect that over 20% of their workforce will need reskilling,
rising above 30% among the highest-performing AI adopters. Early-career
workers in highly AI-exposed occupations show a more pronounced relative
decline in entry-level hiring than experienced workers in the same
occupations, per Stanford Digital Economy Lab research — indicating
organisations are adjusting hiring pipelines before touching existing
headcount.
